Case Study — AI Voice Training System

From Sprint to System: How BaySys Built a Voice AI Trainer for 18,000 Calls a Day

96 hours to first call. 18 months of builds. 242 agents, 45 AI personas, 420 tests, and a daily eval pipeline that never sleeps.

BaySys.ai April 2026 18 min read

The problem emerged in the data before it surfaced in the calls. BaySys.ai manages roughly ₹6,600 crore (approximately $700 million) in distressed consumer debt across 650,000 customer accounts with a 16-person headquarters team. The operation places over 18,000 calls per day to debtors across India through UVARCL, their collection arm. New agents, placed into this volume without structured preparation, fail their first real conversations not because they lack basic phone etiquette but because they have never explained to a sceptical debtor why a debt buyer now owns their account, navigated accusations that the entire call is a scam, or held composure when a customer dissolves into emotional distress about missing medical treatment costs. There was no safe place to practice. No simulated environment where failure was expected and coached. A new agent's first real call is their first real failure.

The traditional answer to this problem is weeks of roleplay training with senior agents, manual scorecards, and subjective feedback delivered inconsistently across multiple geographies and agencies. The constraint was absolute: BaySys could not pause its collections operation to run multi-week training cycles. And the method itself was unsuitable. Agents trained in a classroom or on a web interface are not trained for the real environment—the ambient noise of a collection centre, the unpredictable responses from actual debtors, the weight of knowing that the customer on the other end of the line is real.

On March 24, 2026, the CTO began building what became the BaySys Voice AI Trainer in a single intensive sprint. The system needed to simulate realistic customer conversations, adapt difficulty on the fly, score automatically over a normal phone line, and produce structured feedback within seconds of call completion. Four engineers run the entire technology operation. The CTO could not tap them. He had to build this alone.

The Architecture

Building the Voice Stack: Orchestration Over Custom Plumbing

Ninety-six hours compressed into four key technical decisions. The first was scope: build from scratch using Twilio plus custom STT/TTS/LLM orchestration, accepting three to four weeks of infrastructure work, or select an existing managed platform that handles orchestration as a product. The trade-off was clear—managed platforms add a margin over direct API costs and inject latency into the audio path. Building raw offers full control but consumes the timeline. The decision was operational: use Bolna.ai as the orchestration layer.

The second decision concerned the language problem. India's distressed debt portfolios span 16+ languages and dialect zones. Agents call debtors in Hinglish, Tamil mixed with English, Marathi, Urdu. Global English-trained text-to-speech models cannot pronounce Hindi correctly. The word मैं (mein, meaning "I") is transliterated as "mein" and pronounced by western TTS systems to rhyme with "rain"—a jarring foreign accent that signals to a native Hindi speaker within milliseconds that this voice is not Indian. Customer trust evaporates before the introduction concludes. This single failure eliminated entire provider categories. The solution required writing regional language text in native script (Devanagari for Hindi, Tamil script for Tamil) within system prompts, forcing the TTS engine to process the correct phoneme. The choice of text-to-speech became ElevenLabs eleven_turbo_v2_5 with the voice "Neha P," female, Indian-accented, with fine-grained control over pronunciation and delivery speed.

The third decision involved the language model. GPT-4.1-mini was selected over Google Gemini 2.0 Flash Lite after testing prompt-following consistency on Hinglish construction. The LLM runs on Azure for data residency. Temperature was set to 0.3 to enforce consistent persona behaviour across repeated calls. Max tokens per turn capped at 300 to enforce the one-to-two-sentence rule: an AI debtor should never monologue. Response length is correlated with agent performance—shorter turns force the agent to listen and adapt, not wait out a soliloquy.

The fourth decision was speech recognition. Deepgram nova-3 was selected for its domain keyword boosting capability (BaySys.ai, EMI, CIBIL, CRILC can be injected as boost terms) and superior Hinglish code-switching accuracy at 8kHz, the bandwidth of PSTN telephony. Noise cancellation was set to 85 to filter the ambient noise of a collection centre floor.

The Persona System

Nine Debtors, Five Levels, 45 Prompts—And the Integrity Guardrails That Keep Them Real

A persona in this system is not a loose character sketch. Each of the nine AI-driven debtors is a fully specified individual: age, occupation, city, loan product, days past due, previous contact history, language preference, and a detailed psychological profile describing how they respond under pressure. The personas were designed by working backwards from real customer profiles in BaySys.ai's portfolio, ensuring every objection, deflection, and emotional beat maps to behaviour the training agents will actually encounter.

Rajesh Gupta is a 43-year-old accounts supervisor in Kanpur, defaulted on a personal loan 238 days past due, speaks Hinglish, and is a polite deferrer who has heard seven prior collection calls without committing. He will ask smart questions about the debt assignment chain—"Who owns this now? How did you get my number? What happens if I don't pay?"—and his scam suspicion is genuine, informed by news stories about fraudsters posing as debt collectors. At difficulty level L5, he becomes actively hostile: refuses to engage, hangs up, blocks numbers. Level L1 makes him receptive, ready to listen, prepared to discuss settlement.

Karthikeyan Subramanian is a 38-year-old IT project manager in Bangalore, 215 days past due on a personal loan, speaks English-Tamil-Hinglish, and is procedurally challenging. He has read RBI circulars about debt collection regulations, has logged the date of the legal notice, demands written communication from every collector (none arrives), and will cite regulatory sections to deflect calls. Imran Sheikh, a textile wholesaler in Hyderabad, 227 days past due, is community-reputation-minded—he will always defer—not from shame but from a concern about how the situation looks to family and neighbours. Sunita Kapoor in Lucknow, a housewife, 215 days overdue, is overwhelmed and family-protective. Her husband does not know the full amount. An agent must navigate her anxiety about financial disclosure without pressing too hard. She will close off entirely if confronted.

The system does not reveal difficulty level to agents. Difficulty is assigned server-side based on performance history. A strong agent receives harder scenarios—hostile debtors, procedural challengers, those with genuine scam suspicion. A struggling agent receives cooperative personas at lower difficulty, scenarios designed to build confidence. Real customers do not announce their mood or cooperation level. Neither does the AI.

The prompt engineering required was substantial. Each persona runs at five difficulty levels (L1 to L5) with a distinct prompt for each combination, generating 45 total prompt rows. The version format follows the pattern L{n}-v{X.Y}, where "L3-v11.1" means difficulty level 3, version 11, minor iteration 1. The current live version across all personas is v11.9. Three Bolna agents handle all nine personas—the system selects which agent based on the voice type required (female, male, south Indian), and injects the persona and difficulty into the system prompt at call initiation time. Portfolio matching ensures agents working Axis Bank personal loan collections always face an Axis Bank scenario during training. The persona never breaks character, never reveals it is being trained, never acknowledges the training context.

The Scoring Rubric

28 Points: Three Domains, 14 Criteria, Mapped to Real Failure Modes

The scoring framework emerged from listening to actual calls where agents failed. Not failed gracefully but failed so completely that customers hung up believing the entire call was a scam. The rubric organizes 14 binary criteria (each scoring 0 or 2 points) into three domains: introduction quality, call quality, and scam-trust handling. Total maximum score is 28 points.

Introduction quality measures the critical first 90 seconds. Did the agent greet appropriately for time of day? Did they introduce themselves by name before asking any question? Did they establish bank and product context? Most critically: did they explain the debt assignment chain—that a debt buyer now owns the account and BaySys.ai is the RBI-licensed collection servicer? This is where most agents fail. They either skip the explanation entirely (customer confusion), over-explain it (customer overwhelm), or explain it with uncertainty (customer distrust).

Call quality measures the conduct of the conversation. No fumbling, laughing, or unprofessional behaviour. No harsh language or unparliamentary tone. Accurate answers to every question. Evidence that the customer is satisfied with the responses. Forward movement in the conversation arc—the call progresses from suspicion to trust to engagement. The call closes with an appropriate remark and clear next action. Regulatory concerns about legitimacy and the legality of the assignment are addressed correctly.

Scam-trust handling addresses the elephant in the call. The agent must explicitly confirm they are not asking for OTP, password, card number, or any sensitive data. The agent must offer an independent verification route—the BaySys.ai helpline, website, RBI registry, checking the credit bureau where the debt buyer's name now appears. This domain carries only 4 points of the 28 maximum, but it is the most critical. A single failure here—an agent asking for any sensitive information or failing to offer verification—is a disqualification.

Scoring is computed server-side from 14 raw binary fields extracted from the Bolna.ai call trace, not extracted from voice AI itself. This prevents a fundamental failure: having two sources of truth about whether criteria were met. The Bolna LLM analyzes the call transcript directly, generates the 14 binary fields as JSON, and those raw fields are summed to produce the 28-point score. Rating bands follow: 24–28 is Excellent, 18–23 is Good, 11–17 is Needs Work, 0–10 is Poor.

The Deployment

From Isolated Build to Embedded CRM System: Three Backend Changes, Five React Components

By Tuesday evening, March 26, the standalone trainer was validated. Agents could dial in via the Bolna interface, receive callbacks from the AI trainer, and scores came back clean. The system worked as proof-of-concept. But a proof-of-concept is not a training system—not yet. Production integration required embedding the trainer into the CRM where agents spend their day. The system needed to gate access: agents must complete Voice Coach scenarios before live practice calls unlock, and warm-up scores from practice calls must meet a threshold before an agent can make real customer calls.

The CRM integration, built into the Django backend and React SPA at crm.bayst.finance/trainer/hub, required three backend changes (INSTALLED_APPS, URL include, environment variables) and five frontend changes (briefing view, call initiation, in-call polling, scorecard display, coaching note rendering). The single seam between development (MockCrmAuth) and production (CrmJWTAuthentication) is crm_adapter.py. All views return JSON—no templates, HTMX, or server-side rendering. Authentication roles are granular: role 1 is cross-agency admin, role 2 is manager/team lead (agency-scoped), role 3 is agent (own records), role 4 is supervisor, role 5 is agency admin.

The entire Django module, including the Bolna webhook integration, was built and tested in a single 8-hour session with 129 passing tests and zero ruff findings. A two-week project compressed to half a day because the architecture was clear, the CRM was well-designed, and the boundary between systems was clean. Bad codebases cannot be built fast. This one could.

The Evaluation Pipeline

Weekly Automated Quality Control: Layer 1 Rules, Layer 2 LLM Reasoning

The eval pipeline runs weekly, automatically, without human intervention. Every call transcript across 2,100+ real conversations from the previous week is scored against seven Layer 1 rule-based checks and analyzed by Layer 2 LLM-based reasoning (currently in development). The checks are designed to prevent regressions as the system evolves.

Layer 1 checks for UVARCL leaks—if the AI persona mentions BaySys.ai or UVARCL before the agent says it, training data integrity is compromised. Bank name leaks—if the AI reveals the bank name before the agent establishes it. Settlement volunteer—if the AI raises settlement or OTS before the agent brings it up. Response length—sentences per assistant turn over three indicates a monologue, a fail. Phase 1 present—checks the AI shows appropriate surprise when UVARCL is first mentioned. Template variable leaks—literal {bank_name} or {difficulty_level} in AI output is an extraction failure. Gender misgendering—for female personas, masculine Hindi verb forms (करता, बोलता) indicate a prompt error.

These checks run daily (python manage.py eval_run) against a smart-sorted priority selection of 50–100 calls. The eval run generates a CallEvalResult record per call and publishes a markdown or JSON report to the eval-reports/ directory. Run ID format is {year}-W{week}. Duplicate prevention ensures no run fires twice without --force flag.

The regression testing system was upgraded from synthetic scripts (5 hand-crafted examples) to replay-against-real-transcripts from T-1, stratified by agency, rating, and caller type. Every prompt change runs 50 diverse transcripts through Layer 1 and behavioural spot-check before production deployment. This is how the system went from "promising" to "safe to depend on."

Team lead view (role 2, agency-scoped) displays agency-specific KPIs, action board, leaderboard, and rating trends. Score distribution (from call history) and agent distribution (by average score) provide visibility. Level distribution shows how many agents are stuck at L1 versus progressing through L5. Stuck alerts flag agents who haven't advanced beyond their current level for 14+ days—Pooja L1, 16 days, needs intervention. Connected calls and PTP conversion percent ranked show Sarita leading conversion at 6.4% on 513 connected calls.

Agent detail view (managers only) shows level progression track (L1→L5 with completed/pending checkmarks), score trend (last 10 calls as line chart with rating band reference lines), performance by section with agency benchmark markers (Introduction 68% agent / 56% agency, Call Quality 88% / 80%, Scam Handling 60% / 30%), call history with audio playback, and expanded scorecards showing all 14 criteria in 3 sections.

The Voice Coach precedes everything else. Five scenarios, two languages (Hindi and English), six phases each. Gate: new agents must complete one scenario before Practice unlocks. The Coach teaches the rubric through structured scenarios, preparing agents for real personas.

The Maintenance

Three Celery Tasks Running Daily: Recordings, Snapshots, Resolution

fetch_recording_urls polls Bolna for completed training calls missing recording URLs. Filters calls longer than 10 seconds (excludes too-short calls). Processes up to 50 per run. Records S3 presigned URLs from Bolna's response. snapshot_rating_distributions runs nightly, snapshotting RatingSnapshot and SectionScoreSnapshot for yesterday's date per agency and system-wide. Computes average intro/quality/scam section scores as percentages, average discipline from CRM adapter, agent count per agency. Enables the Trends chart (8-week view populated nightly). resolve_stuck_calls polls Bolna for calls stuck in "initiated" or "ringing" status, resolves them to failed/no_answer if Bolna confirms they ended.

420+ Passing Tests: From Initial Scaffold to Stable Architecture

The test suite totals 420+ passing tests with zero ruff findings as of April 2026. Each migration solved a real problem discovered in production or testing. This is how systems evolve from prototypes to platforms—one problem, one migration, one test, at a time.

Test coverage includes models, services, serializers, views, webhook handling, initiate flow, CRM adapter, dashboard enrichments, telemetry, and backfill operations. The test suite is not a checkbox—it is the operational contract that ensures the system does what it claims.

The Outcome

What Was Actually Built

As of April 2026, the system stands at scale: 3 Bolna agents live, 9 personas times 5 levels equals 45 per-level prompts at version 11.9. Two hundred and forty-two agents across 5 agencies training daily. Three thousand four hundred thirty calls this month. Platform average score 63%. The system trained 250 agents in their first week with measurable score improvement of 15–20 points average by week two. Four hundred twenty tests passing. Eighteen migrations from initial scaffold through stable architecture. Five weekly automated eval runs, 2,100+ transcript base. Difficulty progression algorithm (2 consecutive Excellent scores promote, 2 consecutive Poor scores demote). Two languages live in the Voice Coach. Three Celery tasks running daily. A PDF export for coaching reports. All of it built, deployed, and running on a schedule.

The system that took 96 hours to build is now a platform that took 18 months to stabilise, test, and harden into something that runs 242 agents through 3,430 practice and assessment calls per month without manual intervention. That compression—from panic sprint to operational steady-state—is what separates a hack from a system.
